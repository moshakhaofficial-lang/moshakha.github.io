Wheel arches are the dirtiest part of a car and the part most people clean last, if at
all. Both of those are mistakes, and the first one causes the most rework.

## Order first: arches before paint

Arches hold packed mud, road grit, brake dust and — in winter — salt. Cleaning them after
the bodywork means blasting all of that back onto panels you've already washed and dried.

Doing them first means any mess you make gets cleaned up by the wash that follows. This
is the same logic as washing wheels before paint, and it's the single biggest time saver
in the whole routine.

## Rinse before you apply anything

Packed debris blocks the cleaner from reaching the liner. Spraying chemical over a layer
of dried mud just wets the mud.

A proper pressure rinse clears the loose material so the cleaner contacts the surface you
actually want cleaned.

## Full-strength APC is genuinely correct here

Arch liners are unpainted plastic, and the metal around them is coated specifically for
this environment. This is one of the few places on a car where aggressive chemistry has
nothing delicate to damage.

Elsewhere in the car, APC strength is exactly what ruins dashboards — the boundaries are
in
[interior detailer vs all-purpose cleaner](/blog/interior-detailer-vs-all-purpose-cleaner/).
In the arches, use it neat.

## Never the paint mitt

This is the rule worth being absolute about. Arch grit is coarse, sharp, and does not
reliably rinse out of a mitt. A mitt that's been in an arch will carry that grit onto
bodywork the next time it's used.

Keep dedicated arch tools, and store them physically apart from paint tools so there's no
possibility of grabbing the wrong one. That separation logic extends to buckets too —
see [is a third wash bucket worth it](/blog/two-vs-three-bucket-method/).

## Rinse up inside, not just at the opening

Cleaner left in the arch holds road grime and runs out onto paint later as streaks. Direct
water up into the liner where the suspension components sit, not merely at the visible
opening.

## Go easy on dressing

A satin trim dressing tidies faded black liners and marginally eases the next clean.

Gloss dressing in an arch is a bad idea for an obvious reason: it's a high-speed spray
environment, and the product slings straight onto your lower paint on the first drive.
Matte or satin, applied thinly, excess wiped off.

The wheels themselves are a separate job with different chemistry — that's in
