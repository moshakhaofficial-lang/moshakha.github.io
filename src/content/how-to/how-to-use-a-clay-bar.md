Claying is the step between washing and protecting, and it is the one most people either
skip entirely or do badly enough to cause damage.

## What claying actually does

A wash removes what is sitting **on** the paint. Claying removes what has **bonded to**
it: brake dust that arrived hot and welded itself in, industrial fallout, rail dust,
tree sap, overspray.

You can feel it. Run your fingertips over a clean panel and it feels smooth. Put your
hand in a thin plastic bag first and the bag amplifies texture — suddenly you can feel
every bonded particle as fine grit.

That grit is what clay pulls out. The bar is a soft resin that grips the protruding part
of each particle and lifts it free as the clay slides past.

## Do the bag test first

**If the paint feels smooth through the bag, do not clay it.** There is nothing to
remove, and claying is a mildly abrasive process. Doing it on schedule rather than on
need is just abrading your clear coat for no reason.

Once or twice a year is right for most cars. A garaged car may go longer; a car parked
under trees or near industry will need it sooner.

## The lubrication rule

This is the entire safety margin of the process.

**The clay must never touch dry paint.** It floats on a film of lubricant, and that film
is what stops the extracted grit from being dragged across the surface. Being sparing
with lubricant is the single most common cause of clay marring.

Use noticeably more than feels necessary. The panel should be visibly wet. If the clay
starts grabbing or squeaking, stop and add more lubricant rather than pushing through.

Plain water does not work — it has almost no lubricity. Use a dedicated clay lube or a
quick detailer.

## Folding, and the dropped-clay rule

The contamination the clay removes does not vanish. It sits **embedded in the face of
the bar**. Keep working with a loaded face and you are dragging that same grit back
across your paint.

Fold the patty in on itself every couple of sections and flatten it again. Look at it —
visible dark specks mean fold now.

And the absolute rule: **if the clay hits the ground, it is finished.** It has picked up
sharp grit that you will never fully see or remove. Tear off a fresh piece. This is why
you only ever use a third of the bar at a time — dropping it costs you very little.

## Chemical first, mechanical second

An iron fallout remover dissolves bonded brake dust chemically, with no contact at all.
Doing that before you clay means the bar has far less to pick up, so fewer passes and
less marring risk.

It is optional. It also makes the whole job faster and safer, which is a good trade for
the price of a bottle.

## What comes after

Claying strips whatever protection was on the paint. The car is now bare clear coat,
which spots easily and re-bonds contamination fast.

**Protect it the same day.** A wax, sealant, or — if you are going further — a
[ceramic coating](/blog/ceramic-coating-guide), which specifically requires
decontaminated paint to bond properly. Claying is a prerequisite for coating, not an
alternative to it.

If you are correcting paint as well, the order is: wash → decontaminate → clay →
[polish](/how-to/how-to-avoid-swirl-marks) → protect. Claying comes before polishing,
never after.
