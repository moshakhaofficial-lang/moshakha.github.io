Swirl marks are the most common paint defect on a daily-driven car, and they are almost
entirely self-inflicted — usually by the washing that was meant to look after it.

## What they actually are

Not one defect. **Thousands of microscopic scratches** in the clear coat, all roughly
the same shallow depth, scattered across the panel.

A scratch scatters light instead of reflecting it cleanly. Under diffuse light — an
overcast day, a garage — you won't see them. Under a point source, like direct sun or a
service-station forecourt light, each scratch throws light back at a different angle and
the whole population lights up at once as a halo around the light source.

That halo is what people call swirl marks. It looks circular because the scratches were
made in circles.

## Where they come from

In rough order of how much damage they do:

**Automatic car washes with brushes.** The single worst thing you can do to paint. Those
brushes have been dragged over hundreds of filthy vehicles and are loaded with embedded
grit. Touchless washes are fine; anything with spinning bristles is not.

**Washing with a sponge.** A flat face traps grit between the sponge and the paint with
nowhere else to put it. This is structural, not a technique problem —
[the comparison is here](/how-to/wash-mitt-vs-sponge/).

**One bucket.** Every dunk contaminates your soap. By the fourth panel you're washing
with gritty water.

**Wiping a dry car.** Removing dust with a dry cloth, or using a "quick detailer" on a
genuinely dusty panel, drags every particle across the paint with no lubrication at all.
This does an extraordinary amount of damage for how harmless it feels.

**Drying badly.** A dropped towel, a towel washed with fabric softener, or a saturated
towel being rubbed rather than pulled. Drying is the second-highest-risk step after
washing.

**Circular motion.** Doesn't create more scratches — makes the ones you create far more
visible.

## Stopping the damage

The habits, in order of impact:

1. **Never use a brush-type automatic wash.** Nothing else on this list matters if you
   do this monthly.
2. **Two buckets with grit guards.** [Full method here](/how-to/two-bucket-car-wash-method/).
3. **A mitt, not a sponge.**
4. **Top to bottom, straight lines, no pressure.**
5. **Never touch a dry, dusty car.** Rinse first, always. If you cannot rinse, leave it
   dusty — dust does far less harm sitting there than being wiped off.
6. **Dry immediately with a clean, high-GSM towel**, laid flat and pulled.

## Removing the ones you already have

Two honest options, and a lot of marketing that blurs them.

### Filling (temporary)

Glazes, cleaner waxes and "scratch remover" products contain fillers that sit in the
scratches and reduce how much they scatter light. The car looks corrected. It isn't —
the fillers wash out over a few weeks and the swirls return unchanged.

This is a legitimate choice if you know what you're buying. It's cheap, safe, and takes
an afternoon. It is not correction.

### Machine polishing (permanent)

Polishing removes a thin layer of clear coat, levelling the surface down to the base of
the scratches. The scratches are genuinely gone.

The cost is that **clear coat is finite**. A typical factory clear coat is very thin, and
each correction removes some of it. A car can only be properly corrected a handful of
times in its life before the coat becomes too thin to protect the paint underneath.

If you're doing it yourself:

- **Use a dual-action polisher, not a rotary.** A DA's oscillating motion resists
  building enough heat to burn through. A rotary can strike through clear coat in
  seconds in inexperienced hands.
- **Start with the least aggressive combination** — finishing pad, finishing polish — and
  step up only if that isn't enough. You cannot put clear coat back.
- **Test on a lower panel**, not the bonnet.
- **The paint must be perfectly clean and decontaminated first.** Polishing a panel with
  grit still on it grinds that grit in under power.

If the car is a lease, or you're unsure, a professional correction is money well spent —
partly for the result, mostly because they'll measure the paint depth first.

## The part people skip

Correcting paint and then washing it the same way as before just resets a clock. The
swirls come back, and you've spent some of a finite resource to get there.

Fix the method first. Then correct, if you still want to.
