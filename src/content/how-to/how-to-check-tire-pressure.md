Tyre pressure is the highest-impact, lowest-effort maintenance item on a car. It affects
braking distance, wet grip, fuel consumption and how fast the tyres wear out — and
almost everyone gets the target number wrong.

## The number on the sidewall is not your target

This is the single most common error.

The figure moulded into the tyre sidewall — typically something like "MAX 51 PSI" — is
**the maximum pressure that tyre can safely contain**. It is a property of the tyre, not
a recommendation for your car.

Your target is on the **placard**: a sticker in the driver's door jamb, sometimes on the
fuel flap or in the glovebox. It's set by the manufacturer for your vehicle's weight and
suspension, and it's frequently different front to rear.

Inflating to the sidewall number over-inflates most cars substantially. The tyre balloons
slightly, the contact patch shrinks, wet grip drops and the centre of the tread wears
out early.

## Cold means cold

The placard figure is a **cold** specification: parked three hours, or driven under a
mile.

Driving heats the air inside the tyre and raises the reading by several PSI. Check warm
and bleed down to the placard number and you'll be meaningfully under-inflated once it
cools — which is the worse error of the two.

## Temperature swings are not leaks

Pressure drops roughly **1 PSI per 10°F** of temperature fall.

A tyre set correctly on a mild autumn afternoon can be several PSI low on the first
proper cold morning, which is why TPMS lights cluster around the first frost. That's
physics, not a puncture. It still needs correcting.

## TPMS is an alarm, not a gauge

Most direct TPMS systems only warn once pressure is around **25% below** target. That's
far past the point where wet braking and tread wear are affected.

Indirect systems — which infer pressure from wheel rotation speed rather than measuring
it — are less precise again.

Treat the light as "you may have a puncture", not as "your pressures are fine until this
comes on". **Check monthly with a gauge.**

## Check the spare

Spares sit unused for years and lose pressure the whole time. A flat spare found at the
roadside is no spare at all.

Space-savers usually need a considerably **higher** pressure than road tyres — often
around 60 PSI. That figure is on the spare's own sidewall, and it's one of the few times
the sidewall is the number you want.

## What you need

A decent gauge and something to add air with. Pencil gauges are cheap and imprecise; a
digital or quality dial gauge is better and still inexpensive.

For adding air, a [portable inflator](/guides/best-tire-inflators) means never depending
on a forecourt air line that's out of order — and it's the same device that gets you
home after a slow puncture.
