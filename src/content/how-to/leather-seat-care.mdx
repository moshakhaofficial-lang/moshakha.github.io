Most advice about leather car seats assumes you have leather. Usually you do not — at
least not in the sense the advice means.

## Start with the water-drop test

Put one drop of water somewhere inconspicuous. **If it beads and sits on top, the surface
is coated** — a polymer layer over either leather or vinyl. **If it darkens and soaks in,
it is uncoated leather.**

Almost every mass-market car built in the last two decades uses a coated surface. That is
not a downside; the coating is what makes the seat survive sunlight, denim dye, and years
of people sliding across it. But it does change what you should put on it.

## Why this changes the product

A conditioner exists to feed oils into porous leather. **A coating has no pores to feed.**
Applied to a coated seat, conditioner sits on the surface, feels slick, and collects dust
— which is why people who diligently condition their seats often find them dirtier faster.

What a coated seat actually needs is a pH-neutral cleaner to lift dirt off the coating,
and a protectant with UV inhibitors to slow the coating's own degradation.

## Spray the brush, not the seat

This is the technique detail that matters most, and it applies to both surface types.

Spraying directly onto a seat drives liquid into perforations and stitch lines, where it
soaks into the foam underneath. Foam holds water for days, and a seat that stays damp
inside is how an interior starts to smell. Spraying onto the brush puts the cleaner
exactly where the agitation happens and nowhere else.

## Vacuum before you introduce any liquid

Grit collects along seams and in perforations. Wiping cleaner across it turns that grit
into an abrasive slurry and grinds it into the coating — the same mechanism that puts
swirl marks in paint, on a surface that cannot be polished afterwards.

Vacuum every seam with a crevice tool first. The full interior order of operations is in
[the interior detailing guide](/blog/perfect-interior-detail/).

## Where coatings actually fail

The driver's side bolster, almost every time. It takes the most contact, the most
abrasion from clothing, and the most repeated flexing. Once the coating wears through
there, no cleaner or conditioner restores it — that is a re-dye or a re-trim job.

Keeping abrasive grit off that bolster does more for the seat's lifespan than anything
you apply after the fact.
