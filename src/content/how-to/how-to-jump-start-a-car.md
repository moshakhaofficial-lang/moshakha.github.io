Jump starting is routine, and it is also the one common car task with a genuine injury
risk. The connection order is not superstition — it exists because of what a flat
battery is doing chemically.

## Why the order matters

A discharged lead-acid battery **vents hydrogen gas**. Hydrogen ignites readily, and the
ignition source is a spark.

The last connection you make is the one that completes the circuit, and completing a
circuit under load produces a small arc. So you make that final connection on an
**unpainted chassis or engine point, deliberately away from the battery** — the spark
happens several inches from the gas rather than directly above it.

That's the whole logic. Positive connections first while the circuit is incomplete;
final negative furthest from the battery.

## The sequence, compressed

**Connecting:** red to dead + · red to donor + · black to donor − · **black to dead
car's chassis earth**

**Disconnecting:** exactly the reverse.

With a lithium jump pack it's the same order — pack **switched off** while you clamp,
switched on once connected, switched off before you unclamp.

## Stop if the battery looks wrong

Cracked case, bulging sides, visible leakage, or a frozen battery in winter: **do not
jump it.** A damaged or frozen battery can rupture under charging current.

This is the one scenario in this whole procedure where the caution is not
box-ticking. Replace the battery instead.

## Modern cars often have designated jump points

If the battery is in the boot or under a seat — increasingly common — the manufacturer
has provided a remote positive terminal under the bonnet and a specific earth point.

Use them. They exist because the manufacturer has decided where it is safe to introduce
current, and on some vehicles connecting elsewhere can trip protection circuitry.

Two minutes with the manual beats guessing.

## Short cranks, not long ones

Five seconds maximum, then thirty seconds' rest.

Starter motors are not rated for continuous use and overheat quickly. Long cranks also
drain whatever charge the donor has managed to transfer.

**If it hasn't started after three or four attempts, stop.** The problem is not simply a
flat battery, and continuing risks the starter. A strong crank that won't catch is fuel
or ignition, not charge.

## Afterwards

Drive for **at least twenty minutes**. Idling barely charges a modern car, because
alternator output at idle is low and standing electrical loads are high.

If it won't restart after that drive, the battery is no longer holding charge. Repeated
jump starting a failed battery just postpones the same morning.

## Carry the pack, and keep it charged

A [jump pack](/guides/best-jump-starters/) means not needing a second car and a stranger,
which is the actual advantage over cables. Its one failure mode is being flat itself —
recharge every three to six months.

The other common roadside failure is a slow puncture, which a
[portable inflator](/guides/best-tire-inflators/) covers. Those two together handle most
of what actually strands people.
