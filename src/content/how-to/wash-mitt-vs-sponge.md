If you take one thing from this page: **the sponge is the problem, not your technique.**

A wash sponge and a wash mitt look like interchangeable tools that do the same job. They
aren't. They differ in one structural way that determines whether your wash removes dirt
or grinds it into your clear coat.

## The actual difference

**A sponge has a flat, closed face.** When you drag it across a dirty panel, it lifts
some grit — and then holds that grit in the thin gap between the sponge and your paint.
There is nowhere else for the particles to go. Every subsequent pass drags them across
the surface under the pressure of your hand.

**A mitt has a deep pile.** Fibres or chenille noodles pull grit *up* into the pile,
away from the contact surface, where it stays suspended until you rinse it out. The
paint never sees most of it.

That's it. That's the whole difference — and it is enough to determine whether a
fortnightly wash routine slowly destroys your finish or preserves it.

## Why swirl marks appear

Swirl marks are not one big scratch. They're thousands of microscopic ones, all roughly
the same depth, catching light from a point source — the sun, or a service-station
forecourt light — and scattering it in a halo.

Two things make them worse:

**Circular motion.** Circles concentrate marring into overlapping arcs that read as
distinct swirls. Straight lines produce scratches that are far less visible.

**Pressure.** The harder you press, the deeper the particle cuts. A sponge encourages
pressure because it doesn't clean well without it. A well-loaded mitt shouldn't need any.

Dark paint doesn't swirl more than light paint. It just shows what's already there.

## What to do if you only own a sponge

Assuming you're not buying anything today:

- Rinse the sponge in clean water **after every single panel**, not every few.
- Use far more shampoo than feels necessary — lubrication is doing the protecting.
- Never press. If dirt isn't coming off, rinse the panel again rather than scrubbing.
- Straight lines only.
- Bin any sponge that has touched the ground.

This reduces the risk. It does not remove it, because the flat face still has nowhere to
put the grit. A mitt costs less than a single bottle of decent shampoo.

## Doing it properly

The steps above cover a full safe wash. The two habits that matter most:

1. **Two buckets.** Rinse bucket, soap bucket, grit guards in both.
2. **Top to bottom.** Cleanest panels first, filthiest last.

Wheels are the exception to every rule — always first, always with separate tools that
never touch paint. Brake dust is metallic and scratches on contact.
