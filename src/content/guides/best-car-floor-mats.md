Floor mats are one of the few interior accessories that do a genuinely protective job:
they keep water, road salt and grit off carpet that is expensive to replace and almost
impossible to fully dry once soaked.

They are also the one accessory in a car with a real safety failure mode.

## The safety issue

**A mat that slides forward can trap your accelerator or block your brake pedal.**

This is not theoretical — it has been the subject of large vehicle recalls. The
mechanism is simple: an unsecured mat migrates forward over months of feet moving
against it, until it bunches under the pedals.

Three rules:

1. **Never stack a mat on top of another mat.** The new one slides on the old one.
2. **Use the factory retention hooks** if your car has them. They exist for this.
3. **Replace any mat that no longer stays put**, regardless of condition.

This is why fit ranks above material here. A cheap universal mat that slides is worse
than no mat at all.

## The three fit tiers

| Tier | What it is | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Universal** | One shape, all cars | Cheapest; slides, poor coverage |
| **Trim-to-fit** | Semi-universal, cut to your footwell | Good fit for moderate money |
| **Laser-measured** | Moulded per model | Best coverage, highest price |

**Trim-to-fit is the value sweet spot** for most people. Cutting along the moulded guide
lines gives you a mat that actually sits in your footwell and doesn't move — which is
most of what the expensive liners buy you.

Cut conservatively. You can always trim more; you cannot put it back.

## All-weather or carpet

Different jobs, and running both seasonally is a reasonable answer.

**All-weather (rubber/TPE)** contains liquid, salt and grit inside raised edges. In
winter, road salt reaching carpet does real corrosion damage to the floor pan
underneath. This is the mat that saves you money.

**Carpet** looks and sounds better, and absorbs rather than contains — which is fine
until it's saturated, at which point it holds moisture against the floor and causes the
musty smell people blame on the car being old.

## The chalky grey problem

Rubber and TPE liners go chalky, and people assume it's age. Usually it's **cleaner
residue dried in the tread channels**, plus UV.

Rinse standing on edge, from the top down, for longer than feels necessary. A
rubber-specific protector slows the UV component.

**Never use tyre shine or a silicone dressing on a mat.** A glossy mat under the pedals
is exactly the hazard described above, arriving by a different route. This is the one
place in a car where shine is actively dangerous.

## How we chose

Specification analysis — material compound, cold-weather flexibility, edge height,
retention design and fit tier — plus aggregated long-term owner feedback with attention
to how compounds hold up after several winters. No lab testing. No brand paid for
placement.
