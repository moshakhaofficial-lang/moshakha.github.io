Sun shade listings compete on percentage claims — "blocks 99% of UV", "reduces interior
temperature by 40 degrees". There's no standard test behind any of those numbers, and the
conditions they'd depend on vary enormously.

Here's what's actually true and checkable.

## What a shade genuinely does

It blocks direct sunlight from landing on the dashboard, steering wheel and front seats.
That's the real benefit, and it's substantial — those surfaces reach temperatures far
above cabin air, which is why a steering wheel can be genuinely painful to hold while the
air inside merely feels hot.

**The cabin air temperature difference is smaller than the surface temperature
difference.** A shaded car is still hot when you get in; the dash and wheel just aren't
scorching, and the interior plastics aren't taking UV damage while you're at work.

That's a worthwhile outcome. It's just not the outcome the percentage claims imply.

## Coverage beats material

This is the one specification you can verify yourself, and it's more predictive than any
reflectivity claim.

A shade that leaves four inches of glass uncovered along the top edge is admitting direct
sunlight onto the dashboard, and it doesn't matter how reflective the covered portion is.
Sunlight through the gap lands on the dash exactly as it would with no shade there at all.

**Measure your windshield before ordering.** Size labels like "large" mean nothing
consistent between brands, and the difference between a compact windshield and a full-size
SUV aperture is enormous.

## The specification nobody lists: whether you'll use it

The most effective sun shade is the one that's actually deployed.

Accordion shades are durable and cheap and store flat — and a significant number of people
stop using them, because folding one in a hot car park with an armful of shopping is
genuinely annoying. An umbrella shade that collapses one-handed in three seconds gets used
every time.

If you've owned a shade before and stopped using it, that's the variable to optimise for,
and it's why pick one leads this list despite having a mechanism that can eventually fail.

## Reflective side out, but don't agonise

Standard advice is reflective side toward the glass, so energy is turned back before it
enters the cabin. The reasoning is sound.

In practice the difference is far smaller than coverage and consistent use. Some
reversible shades deliberately offer a dark side for winter, when you want the opposite
effect.

## How we chose

Specification analysis — actual dimensions against common windshield apertures,
construction layers, deployment mechanism and storage format — plus aggregated long-term
owner feedback weighted toward whether people were still using the shade a year later. No
lab testing, and we don't repeat unverifiable temperature-reduction claims.

## The related problem: glare from a glossy dash

If you're buying a shade because your dashboard is baking, it's worth knowing that a
glossy dashboard dressing makes the other summer problem worse — reflecting low sun up
into the windshield as a haze across your view.

Matte protectant, applied thinly, avoids it. That's covered in
