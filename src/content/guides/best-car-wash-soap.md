Car shampoo is the cheapest part of a wash and one of the easiest to get wrong — mostly
by reaching for whatever's under the kitchen sink.

## Which one, in one line

| If this is you | Buy |
|---|---|
| Normal car, washed every week or two | **Mr. Pink 64oz** — the default, and the one most people should stop at |
| Two cars, or you wash weekly | **Mr. Pink 128oz** — same formula, far lower cost per wash |
| Older paint, no wax on it | **Gold Class** — conditioners make tired paint look better |
| No time to wax separately | **Extreme Bodywash & Wax** — light gloss during the wash |
| Ceramic-coated car | **Mr. Pink**, and skip anything with wax in it (see below) |

## How long a bottle actually lasts

This is the number that decides value, and almost no listing states it plainly.

Mr. Pink dilutes at **1 to 3 oz per five-gallon bucket**. Call it 2 oz for a normal wash,
and a 64oz bottle gives you roughly **32 washes**. The 128oz jug gives you around **64**.

At a fortnightly wash, one gallon jug is comfortably over a year of washing a single car.
That's the actual argument for the bulk pick — not the shelf price.

Gold Class is less concentrated, so expect to pour noticeably more per bucket. It's
cheaper per bottle and closer to level once you account for how much you use.

The failure mode in both directions is worth naming: **too little shampoo is a paint
risk**, because lubrication is the thing protecting your clear coat during contact. Too
much is only a waste of money. If you're unsure, pour more.

## Why dish soap ruins paint protection

Dish soap is engineered to strip oils and grease. That's exactly what it does to your
wax or sealant, in a single wash, along with drying out rubber seals and plastic trim
over time.

It also has no lubricity. Car shampoo is slick on purpose — that slickness is what
allows a trapped particle to slide across the panel instead of cutting into it. Dish soap
cleans and offers nothing on that front, which makes swirl marks more likely even with
good technique.

There is one legitimate use: deliberately stripping old wax before polishing or applying
a new sealant. If that's not what you're doing, use car shampoo.

## What pH-neutral means

A pH-neutral shampoo lifts dirt without chemically attacking whatever protection is on
the paint. Strongly alkaline cleaners strip wax and sealant — genuinely useful when you
want a clean slate before correction, and destructive when you've just spent an afternoon
waxing.

Every pick above is pH-neutral and safe over wax, sealant and ceramic coatings.

## If your car is ceramic coated

Two things change, and the second one surprises people.

**pH-neutral stops being a preference and becomes a requirement.** A coating is more
chemically durable than wax, but repeatedly washing it with something alkaline degrades
its hydrophobic behavior faster than it should.

**Skip the wash-and-wax.** Pick four is a good product and the wrong one here — the
carnauba sits on top of the coating and masks the water behavior you paid for. When that
layer wears unevenly, people read it as the coating failing and start troubleshooting a
problem they created. Plain pH-neutral shampoo, and a coating-specific topper if you want
one.

## What to avoid, even when it's sold as car soap

Being marketed for cars doesn't make something safe over your protection:

- **Anything labeled a "bug and tar" or "heavy duty" wash.** These are deliberately
  alkaline to cut bonded contamination. Fine as a targeted pre-treatment, destructive as
  your weekly shampoo.
- **Strip wash products**, which do exactly what the name says. Correct before applying
  new protection, wrong at any other time.
- **Bargain concentrates with no stated dilution.** If a bottle won't tell you how much to
  use per bucket, you can't work out cost per wash or whether you're under-lubricating.

## Will it work in a foam cannon?

Mr. Pink does, and that's genuinely useful — one bottle covers both the pre-foam and the
contact wash rather than buying two products.

Gold Class foams less impressively through a cannon, being less concentrated. It's a
bucket shampoo first.

Worth being clear that a foam cannon and a shampoo are solving different problems, and a
dedicated snow foam is formulated for dwell rather than lubrication — that distinction is
in [snow foam vs car shampoo](/blog/snow-foam-vs-car-shampoo/), and the technique is in
[the foam cannon guide](/how-to/foam-cannon-guide/).

## The honest ranking of what matters

Shampoo is genuinely the least important variable in whether your wash damages paint.
In order of actual impact:

1. **Not using a brush-type automatic wash**
2. **[Two buckets with grit guards](/how-to/two-bucket-car-wash-method/)**
3. **[A mitt rather than a sponge](/how-to/wash-mitt-vs-sponge/)**
4. **Washing top to bottom in straight lines**
5. **Drying properly and immediately**
6. Shampoo choice

Any pH-neutral shampoo used with good technique beats the most expensive bottle on the
shelf used with one bucket and a sponge. Buy a decent one, then spend your attention on
the steps above it.
