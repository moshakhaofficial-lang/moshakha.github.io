Detailing brush sets are sold on piece count. The number that matters is how many
genuinely different jobs the set covers — and which surfaces each brush must never touch.

## Boar versus synthetic

These get framed as competing options. They're complementary.

**Natural boar bristle** is soft, holds product well, and is safe near paint and trim.
It's what you want for vents, badges, panel gaps and emblems — anywhere a slip would put
a brush against clear coat.

**Synthetic bristle** is generally stiffer and more chemical-resistant, which makes it
right for engine bays, wheels, and anywhere you're using aggressive cleaner that would
degrade natural bristle over time.

Most people who detail regularly own both, and use the boar set far more often.

## The ferrule is the part that scratches

Worth checking before you buy anything: the **ferrule** is the band where the bristles
meet the handle. On cheap brushes it's bare metal.

Press a brush flat against a surface and the bristles splay, which brings that band closer
to the paint than you intended. A rubberised or coated ferrule removes the risk entirely,
and it's the detail that separates a brush safe near paint from one that isn't.

## Where drill brushes are genuinely fine

Drill brushes get sold as a whole-car solution and they're nothing of the sort. But
dismissing them is wrong too, because on the right surfaces they save real work.

**Fine:** rubber floor mats, wheel arch liners, door sills, load area plastics, engine bay
covers. Robust, unprotected surfaces where speed is the only consideration — the same
logic as using full-strength cleaner in the arches, covered in
[cleaning wheel arches](/how-to/how-to-clean-wheel-wells/).

**Not fine:** paint, alloy wheel faces, upholstery, any coated or soft-touch surface. A
drill delivers far more torque than your hand, and dwelling in one place removes material
rather than dirt.

The failure mode isn't gradual. It's a visible mark in a couple of seconds.

## Keep wheel brushes away from paint

This is the same discipline as wash mitts, for the same reason. Brake dust is metallic,
sharp, and embeds in bristles where rinsing won't reliably clear it. A brush that's been
on a wheel will carry that grit onto whatever it touches next.

Separate brushes, stored separately. The reasoning is in
[wheel and rim care](/blog/wheel-rim-care/).

Specification analysis — bristle material and stiffness, ferrule construction, size range
across the set, and what each brush is safe to contact — plus aggregated owner feedback

## What a sensible kit looks like

You don't need five sets. For most people:

- **A graduated boar set** for trim, vents and badges — the one you'll reach for most.
- **One upholstery brush** if you have fabric seats.
- **A dedicated wheel brush**, kept separate from everything above.
- **A drill brush**, only if you regularly clean rubber mats and arch liners.

Anything beyond that is duplication. The set that covers four genuinely different jobs
beats the ten-piece box that covers one job ten times.
