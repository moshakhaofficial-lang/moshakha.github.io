A dash cam is the one device where the storage fails more often than the hardware, and it
fails in the worst possible way: quietly.

## Why the failure is silent

An ordinary microSD card is designed for the workload a camera or phone gives it — write
some files, read them back, occasionally delete. A dash cam does something completely
different. It writes continuously, fills the card, then overwrites the oldest footage and
keeps going, every minute the car is running.

That cycle wears out flash memory. And when a worn card starts failing, **the camera
usually doesn't tell you.** The recording light stays on. Files still appear. It's only
when you go looking for a specific clip — after the incident that made you want it — that
you find corrupt files, missing segments, or a card the camera can no longer read.

There is no warning stage. That's the entire argument for spending more on the card than
feels proportionate.

## Endurance is a durability spec, not a speed one

"High endurance" describes how much total data the card can absorb over its life, and the
flash type used to get there. It's a different claim from the speed ratings on the front.

Both matter, for different reasons:

- **Endurance** determines whether the card survives two years of looping.
- **Sustained write speed** (U3 / V30) determines whether frames drop while recording.

A fast card that isn't endurance-rated wears out. An endurance card that's only U1-rated
can't keep up with 2K footage. You want both, and the ratings are printed on the card
itself.

## The U1 versus V30 trap

This catches people out because the product names barely change between generations.

**U1 guarantees 10MB/s sustained write. U3 and V30 both guarantee 30MB/s.**

For a 1080p camera, U1 is genuinely adequate. For 2K, 4K, or anything recording at 60fps,
it isn't — and the failure mode is dropped frames, which you won't see until you're
looking for a number plate that only appeared in the frames that went missing.

Pick five below is a deliberate inclusion of the older U1 version, because it's still
widely sold under a name almost identical to the current one. Check the card face, not
the listing title.

## Capacity is about channels, not resolution

The instinct is to size the card by video quality. The bigger variables are how many
cameras are writing and whether parking mode is on.

| Setup | Reasonable size |
|---|---|
| Single 1080p channel | 128GB |
| Single 2K channel | 128–256GB |
| Front + rear channels | 256GB |
| Any setup with parking mode | 256–512GB |

Parking mode is the one that changes the calculation most, because the camera is writing
while you're not there — see
[how to install a dash cam](/how-to/how-to-install-a-dash-cam/) for what enabling it
actually requires.

## How we chose

Specification analysis — endurance rating, guaranteed sustained write class, capacity
against realistic write loads — plus aggregated long-term owner reports of card failure
in continuous-recording use. No lab testing, no paid placement.

## Treat the card as a consumable

Even a properly rated card is wearing out from the day you fit it. Two habits cost
nothing and prevent most of the disappointment:

- **Format it in the camera** every month or two, not on a computer. The camera writes
  the file layout it expects, and formatting clears the fragmentation that builds up
  under constant looping.
- **Replace it every year or two** of continuous use, or sooner if the camera starts
  reporting errors. A card that needs formatting twice is telling you something.

similar reasons, sensor quality matters more than the resolution figure on the box.
