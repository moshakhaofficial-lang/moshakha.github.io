A portable inflator is one of the cheapest genuinely useful things you can keep in a
car, and it is more likely to be the thing that gets you home than the spare wheel is.

Most punctures are slow leaks from a nail or a corroded valve, not blowouts. A slow leak
means you can reinflate and drive to a garage. That is a far better outcome than
changing a wheel on a hard shoulder, and many new cars no longer carry a spare at all.

## Read this before you buy anything corded

**Check whether your car's 12V socket stays live with the ignition off.**

Many modern cars cut power to the cigarette-lighter socket when the ignition is off. A
corded inflator in that car means running the engine to use it — acceptable at a
roadside, genuinely unsafe in a closed garage.

Test it in thirty seconds: plug in a phone charger, turn the car off, see if it still
charges. If it doesn't, buy cordless.

This single check prevents the most common complaint about every product in this
category, and almost no roundup mentions it.

## Auto shut-off is the feature that matters

Set the target pressure and the compressor stops there by itself.

Without it you are watching a small gauge on a vibrating, noisy device and guessing when
to stop — which is how tyres end up over-inflated. Over-inflation reduces the contact
patch, hurts wet grip and wears the centre of the tread.

Every pick here has it. Treat it as a requirement rather than a feature.

## Where the built-in gauge misleads you

Every inflator's gauge reads pressure **at the compressor**, not inside the tyre. Hose
length and fitting resistance mean the two differ, usually by a couple of PSI.

For topping up, that's fine. If you want an accurate figure, let the compressor stop,
disconnect, and check with a separate gauge. This is also why two inflators can disagree
and both be working correctly.

## What pressure to actually use

**The placard in the driver's door jamb**, not the number on the tyre sidewall.

The sidewall figure is the tyre's *maximum safe* pressure. Inflating to it is a common
and consequential mistake. The correct pressure for your car is on the placard, and it
differs front to rear on many vehicles.

Check cold — driving heats the air and raises the reading by several PSI.

full routine.
