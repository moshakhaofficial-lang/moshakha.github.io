Glass is the one interior surface where the result is binary. A dashboard can be
80 percent clean and look fine. A windshield that is 80 percent clean has a streak
across your sightline and you will stare at it every day until you fix it.

## Ammonia is the specification to check

Most household glass cleaner is ammonia-based, and it works well on windows at home.
In a car it causes two problems.

The first is tint. Aftermarket window film is a layered construction with adhesive and
dye layers, and ammonia degrades both. That is the mechanism behind film that goes purple
and starts lifting at the edges. If your car has factory-tinted glass — where the color
is in the glass itself — you are safe, but most darkened side and rear windows on cars
are aftermarket film, and once it is damaged the only fix is removal and replacement.

The second is slower and affects every car. Ammonia attacks vinyl and rubber. Overspray
onto a dashboard, door cards and window seals does cumulative damage over years, which
nobody attributes to the glass cleaner because it happens too gradually to notice.

Ammonia-free removes both problems. That is the entire case for buying an automotive
glass cleaner rather than taking the bottle from under the kitchen sink.

## The film, and why aerosol helps

Interior glass does not get dirty the way exterior glass does. It gets oily.

Plasticizers outgas continuously from the dashboard, door cards and every soft-touch
plastic in the cabin, and they condense onto the coldest surface available, which is the
inside of the glass. Add exhaled moisture, any smoke, and whatever an air freshener is
putting into the air. The result is a thin oily haze that is almost invisible head-on and
glaringly obvious when the sun hits it at an angle.

A dry towel pushes that film around. A weak cleaner dissolves part of it and smears the
rest. What actually works is enough solvent held in contact with the film long enough to
break it down, and that is where the aerosol earns its place — the mist is fine and even,
so it sits on the glass instead of running down it in rivulets.

## Where it falls short

Overspray is the real cost of the aerosol format. An aerosol does not stop at the edge of
the glass; it reaches the dashboard, the A-pillar trim, the infotainment screen and the
door cards. Screens are the one that matters — their anti-glare and oleophobic coatings do
not react well to solvents.

The fix is simple and you should treat it as mandatory: spray the towel, then wipe the
glass. You lose a little of the even-mist advantage and you keep the product off
everything you did not aim at.

It is also expensive per ounce next to a concentrate you dilute yourself. For most
people that is irrelevant — a can lasts months — but if you clean several cars regularly,
a concentrate is the cheaper route.

## The two-towel rule

Almost every complaint about a glass cleaner streaking is actually a towel problem.

Use two. One to clean with, one dry to buff behind it. A single towel gets progressively
wetter and dirtier, and past a certain point it is redistributing dissolved film rather
than removing it. Use waffle-weave or a dedicated low-pile glass towel — plush towels
hold too much product and lint onto glass.

And work the inside of the windshield in horizontal strokes and the outside in vertical
ones, or vice versa. When a streak appears you will immediately know which side it is on.
