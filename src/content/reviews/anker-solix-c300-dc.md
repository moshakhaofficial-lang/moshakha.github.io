Power stations are compared almost entirely on watt-hours, and watt-hours describe what
goes in rather than what comes out. This unit is built around that distinction.

## The conversion loss nobody mentions

The cells inside any power station store DC. Your phone, your camera battery, your
USB-C laptop all want DC.

An AC outlet sits in between, and it is not free. The unit has to invert DC to mains-style
AC, which costs energy as heat. Then your device's own charger converts that AC back down
to DC, which costs more. You have paid a conversion tax twice to get electricity from a
battery into another battery.

Charging straight from a DC or USB output skips both steps. The energy goes from cell to
device without a round trip through AC, and more of the rated capacity ends up where you
wanted it.

That is the entire argument for this variant, and it is a real engineering point rather
than marketing. It is also a modest effect — a slice of capacity recovered, not a
transformation. Our
[portable power station guide](/guides/best-portable-power-stations/) ranks on that basis.

## Where this sits against its siblings

Three units in this category, three different arguments, which is worth laying out because
they are genuinely distinct:

- **[Jackery Explorer 300 Plus](/reviews/jackery-explorer-300-plus/)** — the case is
  chemistry and weight. LiFePO4 in a package light enough to actually get carried.
- **[EcoFlow River 2 Max](/reviews/ecoflow-river-2-max/)** — the case is recharge speed.
  A unit that refills in an hour can be topped up during a lunch stop, which changes how
  much of its capacity you get across a trip.
- **Anker SOLIX C300 DC** — the case is delivery efficiency. Less lost between the cells
  and your devices.

Those are three different ways of getting more useful energy out of a similar battery, and
which matters depends on what you are charging and how you travel.

## Know which variant you are buying

The most important practical warning about this product is not about performance.

Anker sells the C300 in more than one configuration, and this one is the DC-focused
variant. If you need household AC outlets — for a laptop with a barrel-plug adapter, a
CPAP with an AC brick, anything with a two-pin plug — this is not the unit, and no amount
of efficiency argument fixes that.

Second, some configurations ship without a wall charger. Check the listing contents before
buying, because discovering it at the start of a trip is a bad time.

## The light panel

Easy to dismiss as a gimmick and it is not.

A power station gets used in exactly two contexts: camping, and something having gone
wrong. In the second case you are often at the roadside, probably in the dark, needing
light and power at the same time. An integrated light panel on the thing you already took
out of the trunk is more useful than a torch you have to find separately.

## What it will and will not run

Comfortable: phones, tablets, cameras, drone batteries, a USB-C laptop, LED lighting, a
small fan, a CPAP that runs from DC.

Not a chance: kettle, microwave, toaster, hair dryer, space heater, coffee machine. Those
work by making heat, and heat draws power on a scale no 288Wh pack sustains regardless of
how efficiently it is delivered.

## One thing to check in your car

If you plan to charge it from the 12V socket while parked, find out whether that socket
stays live with the ignition off. Many do, and a power station drawing from a parked car
overnight will flatten the car's own battery — which is a worse problem than the one you
were solving.
[Does the 12V socket stay on with the ignition off](/blog/does-12v-socket-stay-on-with-ignition-off/)
explains how to test it.

## Should you buy it?

**Yes, if** your load is genuinely USB and DC — phones, cameras, a USB-C laptop, lights —
and you would rather not pay the inverter tax on every charge.

**No, if** you need household outlets. Buy an AC-equipped unit and accept the conversion
loss, because a unit that cannot power your device is not efficient, it is useless.
