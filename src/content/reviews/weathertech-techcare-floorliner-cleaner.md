This is a cleaner for a specific material, and the case for it rests entirely on one
property: what it does not leave behind.

## The slippery mat problem

Floor mats are the one place in car care where getting the finish wrong is a safety
issue rather than a cosmetic one.

A rubber or TPE liner that has been treated with anything silicone-based — a tire
dressing, a gloss protectant, a general-purpose interior shine product — becomes
slippery. Your shoe is now sliding on the surface your foot braces against to operate the
brake. And a slick mat is also more likely to migrate, which is the other half of the
problem.

This is the reason a rubber-specific cleaner is worth a separate bottle. The requirement
is not better cleaning; it is a matte, non-slip result. TechCare delivers that, and its
protector step is formulated to offer UV resistance without gloss.

If you remember one thing about floor mats: never put a shine product on them. Our
[floor mats guide](/guides/best-car-floor-mats/) covers the retention and safety side in
more detail, because a sliding mat is the genuine hazard in this category.

## Why liners go gray, and what actually fixes it

Everybody with all-weather liners eventually notices them turning a dull chalky gray,
usually blamed on sun or age. Two things cause it, and the first is fixable for free.

**Dried cleaner residue.** All-weather liners have deep tread channels designed to hold
water and grit. Those channels also hold soap, and if you do not rinse thoroughly, the
residue dries to a pale film across the whole surface. This looks identical to permanent
UV degradation and it is not — it rinses out if you are genuinely thorough. Most people
are not, because the liner looks clean while it is wet.

Rinse far longer than feels necessary, and check the result after it has dried rather than
while it is still wet.

**Genuine UV degradation.** The rest of it is real. Sunlight breaks down the surface of
the compound, which is why the driver's side and anywhere exposed to a window fades
first. A rubber protector slows this. It cannot reverse what has already happened.

## Where it holds up

The two-step structure is sound for the same reason it is sound on leather: clean first,
then protect, so you are not sealing grime under a protective layer.

It also works on cargo liners and trunk mats, which is where the bottle size stops looking
excessive. A trunk liner takes a lot of abuse and nobody thinks to clean it until something
leaks back there.

## Where it falls short

It is branded pricing. A generic rubber and vinyl cleaner from an auto parts store does
substantially the same job for less, provided you check two things: that it is not
silicone-based, and that it does not promise a shine. Those two checks are the entire
specification. If a cheaper bottle passes them, buy the cheaper bottle.

It is also wrong for carpet. Carpet mats need a fabric cleaner and ideally extraction, and
if the problem is white salt bloom from winter roads, that has its own fix —
[how to remove salt stains from carpet](/how-to/how-to-remove-salt-stains-from-carpet/).

## Should you buy it?

**Yes, if** you have WeatherTech or similar TPE liners you want to keep looking right, and
you would rather buy the matched product than evaluate ingredient lists.

**No, if** you are happy reading a label — a generic non-silicone rubber cleaner with no
gloss claim does the same work for less. Either way, the thing that matters is what you do
not use: keep every shine product out of the footwell. If you are still choosing liners,
[the trim-to-fit mats](/reviews/weathertech-trim-to-fit-mats/) are the cheaper route to a
proper fit.
