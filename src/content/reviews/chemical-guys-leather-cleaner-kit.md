Leather care is the part of interior detailing with the most inherited folklore attached
to it. Most of that folklore is about furniture leather and saddlery, and it stopped
applying to cars around the time manufacturers started coating everything.

## What your seats actually are

Unless you are driving something genuinely exotic, the leather in your car is finished
with a polyurethane coating. The hide is underneath, but what you touch is a flexible
plastic layer printed with a grain pattern.

This matters because it determines what every product can possibly do. Oils and waxes
cannot reach the hide — the coating is a barrier, which is exactly its job. Anything
marketed as feeding, nourishing or restoring the leather is describing a mechanism that
does not exist on a coated seat.

What a conditioner genuinely does is keep the coating itself pliable, reduce the squeaky
dry feel, and even out appearance. That is a real and worthwhile outcome. It just is not
the outcome on the label.

Our [leather seat care guide](/how-to/leather-seat-care/) goes through the full process.

## Why the two-step order is the product

This is the actual argument for buying a kit rather than a single spray.

Dirt on a leather seat is not sitting on top of the coating. It works into the grain
texture — the printed valleys that make the plastic look like hide — and into the
perforations if your seats are ventilated. Body oils bind it there.

Apply a combined clean-and-condition product and you lift some of that soil and seal the
rest under a fresh conditioner layer. Now the grime is held against the coating, and every
time you slide across the seat it works as a very mild abrasive. Over years, that is a
meaningful part of why driver's seat bolsters wear faster than the passenger side.

Clean first with a dedicated cleaner, wipe away what you lifted, then condition the clean
surface. It is one extra pass and it is the difference between maintaining the seat and
slowly polishing dirt into it.

## Where it holds up

The pH balance claim is worth something. A cleaner too far either side of neutral attacks
the coating, and alkaline household cleaners are a common cause of coating failure on
seats — people reach for an all-purpose cleaner, it works impressively the first time,
and the coating starts hazing a year later.

The non-greasy finish is the other thing worth paying for. A conditioner that leaves the
seat slick is unpleasant to drive on — you slide under braking — and it attracts dust that
then shows against the sheen. This leaves the seat matte and dry to the touch.

Two 16oz bottles is also enough product for several complete interiors, which makes the
per-use cost low even though the kit costs more than a single spray.

## Where it falls short

It is slower. Two bottles, two passes, two towels, and on perforated seats a soft brush
to work the cleaner out of the perforations without forcing it through into the foam. If
you want a five-minute wipe-down, this is not it.

And it cannot fix damage. Cracking, wear that has gone through to the hide, dye transfer
from denim that has migrated into the coating — none of that is a cleaning problem. A
cleaner and conditioner maintain a coating that is intact. Once it is not, you need
leather repair.

## Should you buy it?

**Yes, if** you have leather seats you intend to keep looking good, and particularly if
the bolsters have started going gray. The two-step approach is the right method and this
is a competent, well-priced execution of it.

**No, if** your seats are vinyl or fabric. Vinyl needs far less and tolerates a general
interior cleaner like
[Total Interior](/reviews/chemical-guys-total-interior-cleaner/); fabric needs an
entirely different approach, covered in
[how to clean fabric car seats](/how-to/how-to-clean-car-seats-fabric/).
