Jump starters are a product you buy once and hope never to need, which makes them easy
to buy badly. The specification that gets pushed hardest is the one that means least.

## Peak amps are not a specification

The GP4000 is sold on a 4000A peak figure. Before anything else, understand what that
number is and is not.

There is no enforced standard for how peak amps are measured in this category. Brands
measure under different conditions, over different durations, and report the result that
flatters the product. The consequence is that peak amp ratings are not comparable between
manufacturers — a conservatively rated 2000A unit from one brand may out-crank an
optimistically rated 4000A unit from another.

What the number does tell you, read loosely, is which tier of the range you are in. A pack
advertising 4000A is a physically large, high-capacity unit, and large high-capacity units
have more margin than small ones. That is a real and useful thing. It just is not a
measurement you can do arithmetic with.

Our [jump starter guide](/guides/best-jump-starters/) ranks on engine size and build
rather than on headline amps, for this reason.

## Who the headroom is actually for

Cranking current demand varies enormously by engine, and the gap is wider than most people
assume.

A warm four-cylinder gas engine is easy. It turns over against low compression with thin
oil, and a compact jump starter handles it repeatedly without strain.

A cold diesel is a different problem. Diesels compress air hard enough to ignite fuel
without a spark, so the starter motor works against far greater resistance. Now add
winter: oil thickens, and the battery itself delivers less current when cold. This is
precisely the scenario where an undersized unit cranks the engine for two seconds, slows,
and stops — and where headroom stops being a luxury.

Large-displacement gas V8s sit between the two and benefit from the margin as well.

So the honest sizing advice: if you drive a diesel, a truck, or something with a big V8,
this tier is the right one. If you drive a Civic, it is more weight than you need, and
weight matters for something that has to live in the car to be useful.

## The power bank function is not a throwaway

High-capacity jump starters carry enough cells that the USB output is genuinely useful
rather than a token feature. This will charge phones repeatedly, run a tire inflator, or
keep a tablet alive on a long drive.

That changes the ownership story. A compact jump starter sits in the trunk untouched for
years and you discover it has self-discharged when you need it. A unit you use as a power
bank gets charged regularly as a side effect, which means it is far more likely to be
ready when a battery actually dies.

## Where it falls short

Bulk and weight are the direct cost of the capacity. Against a compact unit like the
[NOCO GB40](/reviews/noco-boost-gb40-jump-starter/), this is a noticeably larger object
to find space for, and that is a real consideration in a small car.

The protection circuitry is the other honest caveat. Reverse-polarity and spark protection
on this unit are competent, and NOCO's reputation in that specific area is better
established over more years and more units. For a device you will use once, in the dark,
possibly flustered, with the manual long gone, that reputation has value. It is not a
reason to avoid the GP4000 — it is a reason not to assume all protection circuits are
equal.

## Storage, which decides whether it works

Two failure modes account for most jump starters that do not work when needed.

The first is self-discharge. Lithium packs lose charge slowly in storage and a unit left
for two years is likely flat. Check it every few months.

The second is heat. A closed car in summer reaches temperatures well beyond ambient, and
heat degrades lithium cells faster than cycling does. Storing a jump starter in a trunk
year-round is the normal thing to do and it does shorten its life.
[Can you leave a jump starter in a hot car](/blog/can-you-leave-a-jump-starter-in-a-hot-car/)
covers the trade-off.

## Should you buy it?

**Yes, if** you drive a diesel, a truck or a big V8, or you want one unit that will start
anything your household owns.

**No, if** you drive a small gas car — buy a compact unit, because the one you keep in the
car beats the one that is too bulky to bother with. And if your battery is the actual
problem rather than bad luck,
[when to replace a car battery](/blog/car-battery-when-to-replace/) is the more useful
read, and
