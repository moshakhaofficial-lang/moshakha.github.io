Portable power stations are sold on watt-hours, and watt-hours are the right headline
number. The specification that changes the experience, though, is one most buyers do not
weigh at all.

## Charge time decides how much capacity you get

Consider two power stations with identical 500Wh capacity. One recharges from empty in
about an hour. The other takes six.

On a single overnight trip they are equivalent. Across a four-day road trip they are not
remotely equivalent, and the difference compounds.

The fast one can be topped up any time you stop somewhere with an outlet — lunch, a coffee
stop, an hour at a visitor center. You can realistically put it back to full several times
across the trip, so you are working with 500Wh repeatedly.

The slow one can only be charged while you sleep somewhere with power. If you are camping
without hookups, that means it gets charged when you happen to be somewhere with an
outlet, which on a lot of trips is once or twice. You have 500Wh, total, for four days.

Same number on the box. Very different amount of usable energy. This is the River 2 Max's
actual selling point, and it is the sort of thing you only notice after owning a slow one.

## What LiFePO4 buys you

The cell chemistry is the other meaningful difference from older power stations.

LiFePO4 cells are rated for substantially more charge cycles than the lithium-ion
chemistry used in earlier units — the kind of difference that separates a unit still at
most of its capacity after years of regular use from one that has noticeably faded. They
also handle heat better, which is relevant for anything that lives in a vehicle, where
summer cabin temperatures are far above ambient.

The cost is weight. LiFePO4 is heavier per watt-hour than lithium-ion, which is part of
why this is a more substantial object than a lithium-ion unit of similar capacity like the
[Jackery Explorer 300 Plus](/reviews/jackery-explorer-300-plus/).

For a power station that travels in a car rather than a backpack, that is usually the
right trade. Our
[portable power station guide](/guides/best-portable-power-stations/) covers where the
lighter option makes more sense.

## What 499Wh actually runs

Worth being concrete, because expectations in this category run high.

It will comfortably handle: phone and laptop charging many times over, camera batteries,
LED lighting, a CPAP machine overnight, a fan, a 12V fridge for a day or more, a drone
or two.

It will not handle: a kettle, a microwave, a toaster, a hair dryer, a space heater, or a
coffee machine with a heating element. Anything that makes heat draws power on a scale
this class of product cannot sustain, and even where the output rating technically allows
it, the pack empties in minutes.

The step up to the 300W-plus sustained output tier does genuinely widen what you can run
compared to the 300Wh class, which is the second reason to pick this over a smaller unit.

## Where it falls short

The fan. Fast charging generates heat and the fan deals with it audibly. If you are
charging in a tent or a quiet room at night, you will hear it. Charging more slowly, where
the unit allows it, keeps things quieter.

Size and weight are the other cost. Against the 300Wh class this is a two-handed object
you find a place for rather than something you toss in a bag.

## Charging from the car

You can charge from the 12V socket while driving, and it is worth understanding what that
gives you: a slow trickle that offsets consumption rather than a meaningful refill. Useful
on a long driving day, not a substitute for AC.

One thing to check before leaving it plugged in overnight — whether your car's 12V socket
stays live with the ignition off. Many do, and a power station charging from a parked car
will flatten the car's battery while you sleep.
[Does the 12V socket stay on with the ignition off](/blog/does-12v-socket-stay-on-with-ignition-off/)
explains how to find out for your car.

## Should you buy it?

**Yes, if** you take multi-day trips where you can only top up briefly, and you want
capacity that is available repeatedly rather than once. The fast recharge is a real
advantage that shows up on exactly those trips.

**No, if** weight is your main constraint, or you never have mains access to charge from —
in which case a lighter unit, or a solar setup, suits you better.
