Batteries rarely fail without warning. They fail after several weeks of warning that gets
rationalised away, usually in a car park on the first properly cold morning.

## The signals, in the order they appear

**Slow, laboured cranking on cold mornings.** The clearest early warning and the most
ignored — the car still starts, so it doesn't feel like a problem. Cold is when the
battery must deliver the most current and can deliver the least, which is why marginal
batteries show themselves there first.

**Headlights dimming noticeably at idle.** Capacity is dropping under load. The charging
system is compensating while running but there's less reserve behind it.

**Needing a jump more than once in a season.** That's past warning. That's a replacement.

## Why repeat jumps make it worse

The jump itself doesn't damage a healthy battery. What does the damage is the **deep
discharge that made the jump necessary.**

Running a lead-acid battery flat permanently reduces its capacity, and each time it
happens the remaining capacity is smaller. So a car being jump started regularly is on a
declining curve, not a stable workaround.

## Get it tested before buying

This matters because the symptoms overlap with two other faults that a new battery won't
fix:

- **A failing alternator** produces very similar behaviour — the battery never gets
  properly recharged, so it presents as a weak battery.
- **Corroded terminals** restrict current flow and mimic low capacity exactly.

Most auto parts stores test batteries and charging systems free in a few minutes. Worth
doing before spending money on the wrong part.

## Heat is harder on batteries than cold

This surprises people, because failures happen in winter.

Sustained heat accelerates the internal degradation that reduces capacity. Cold doesn't
cause that damage — it **exposes** it, by demanding peak current from a battery that's
already diminished.

So a hot summer is what wears the battery out, and the first cold morning is what tells
you.

## Short journeys shorten life

A battery that's never fully recharged sits in a partial state of charge, which
accelerates sulphation and permanent capacity loss.

If most of your driving is short trips, that's a genuine factor in why batteries don't
last as long as the numbers suggest — and an occasional longer run, or a maintainer,
addresses it.
