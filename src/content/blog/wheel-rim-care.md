Wheels are the dirtiest part of a car and the one most likely to cause paint damage —
not directly, but through the tools you use on them.

## The rule that matters most

**A tool that has touched a wheel never touches paint again.**

Brake dust isn't ordinary dirt. It's metallic particles worn off the pads and rotors,
often hot enough on contact to partially bond to whatever they land on. Those particles
embed in a mitt or brush and stay there through rinsing.

Drag that mitt across a door panel and you're scratching paint with tiny pieces of metal.
This isn't a risk to be managed with care — it's a reason for permanent separation:

- A **dedicated wheel mitt or brush**, never used elsewhere
- A **separate bucket**, or wheels done entirely before your wash buckets are set up
- **Separate towels**, marked so they can't drift back into the paint pile

Snipping a corner off retired wheel towels is a simple mark that survives laundering.

## Wheels first, always

They're the filthiest part of the car, and cleaning them sprays contamination onto the
lower panels and arches. Doing wheels first means the body wash that follows removes
whatever splashed.

Clean them one at a time, and rinse each before the cleaner dries — most wheel cleaners
are far more difficult to remove once they've dried on.

## Choosing a cleaner

**pH-neutral is the safe default.** It's effective on normal brake dust and won't harm
factory painted, clear-coated alloys.

**Acidic cleaners** are aggressive and appropriate only for severe, bonded contamination
— and only on finishes you've confirmed tolerate them. They can permanently etch
polished, anodised and bare metal, and repeated use dulls clear coat.

**Iron-removing fallout cleaners** dissolve bonded brake dust chemically rather than
through scrubbing. They're the right answer for wheels that look dirty no matter how hard
you scrub, and they're gentle in use — the colour change as they react is iron
dissolving, not damage.

**If you don't know the finish**, start pH-neutral. You can escalate; you can't
un-etch.

## Getting into the barrel

The face is easy. The inner barrel holds most of the brake dust and is what makes wheels
look grey from behind.

A long-handled soft brush or a small wheel mitt reaches it. Do this properly and wheels
stay clean far longer, because the barrel is where contamination accumulates and
redistributes from.

Lug nut recesses and around the valve stem need a small detailing brush.

## Tyres and arches

Do these **after** the wheel face, not before — tyre cleaner splashed onto a clean wheel
means redoing it.

Scrub the tyre sidewall with a stiff brush and an all-purpose cleaner. Old dressing
builds up as a brown film that "browns" a few days after each application; removing that
build-up is what makes a new dressing look right.

Apply dressing to an applicator, not directly to the tyre, and wipe off the excess.
Overapplied dressing slings onto your clean paint the first time you drive.

## Protecting them

A wheel sealant or ceramic coating makes the next clean dramatically easier — brake dust
sits on the coating instead of bonding to the wheel. It's one of the higher-return
protective steps available, because wheels get far dirtier than paint does.

Apply to genuinely clean, decontaminated, dry wheels or it seals contamination in.
