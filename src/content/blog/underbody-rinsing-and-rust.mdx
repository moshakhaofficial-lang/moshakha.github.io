Everyone washing a car in winter is doing it for the paint. The part of the vehicle that
actually rusts is the part nobody rinses.

## Where the salt actually sits

**Wheel arch liners and their edges.** Packed with salted slush that stays damp for days
after the visible surfaces have dried. The worst location by a clear margin.

**Sills, seams and subframe ledges.** Horizontal surfaces that collect residue rather than
shedding it, often with a lip that holds moisture against the metal.

**Behind bumpers and under the boot floor.** Enclosed, unventilated, and completely
invisible until something rusts through from the inside.

None of these get touched by a normal wash. All of them stay wet long after the panels
have dried.

## Why salt accelerates rust

Two mechanisms, both worth understanding because they explain why rinsing works.

Salt is **hygroscopic** — it attracts and holds moisture, so a salted surface stays damp
far longer than a merely wet one. And dissolved salt lowers the electrical resistance of
that moisture, which speeds up the electrochemical reaction corrosion actually is.

Rinsing removes both effects at once. That's why it's more effective than anything you
could apply on top.

## Frequency beats thoroughness

This is the practical point.

A quick weekly rinse during salting season does considerably more than one careful session
a month. The goal isn't a clean underbody — it's **limiting how long salt stays in
contact.**

That reframing makes the job achievable. You're not crawling under the car; you're
directing water up into the arches and along the sills for a couple of minutes.

## What to avoid spraying directly

The underbody handles road spray constantly, so water isn't the risk. Concentrated
pressure at close range is.

Avoid aiming a narrow jet directly at wiring connectors, ABS sensors, the rubber boots on
suspension joints, and the edges of underbody trim panels. Spread spray, sensible
distance, and none of that matters.

## Underseal isn't a substitute

Rustproofing coatings help on sound metal, but they're a complement to rinsing rather than
a replacement.

The failure worth knowing about is coating applied over existing corrosion, or in a way
that traps moisture behind it — which accelerates the problem rather than slowing it.
Rinsing carries no such risk and costs nothing.
