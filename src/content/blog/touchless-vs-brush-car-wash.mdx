The usual advice is to avoid automatic washes entirely and hand wash everything. That's
correct in principle and unrealistic for a lot of people in February, so it's worth
understanding what each option actually costs.

## Brush washes: the risk is physical

The media that touches your car touched the car ahead of you, and the one before that.
Whatever grit came off those vehicles is what's now being dragged across your paint.

That's the mechanism behind the swirl marks that make dark cars look hazy under direct
sun — the same mechanism as washing with a dropped mitt, repeated across every panel.

Modern closed-cell foam and soft-cloth systems are genuinely better than the old nylon
brushes, and well-maintained sites rinse their media between vehicles. The problem is that
**you can't tell which kind you're in until afterwards.**

## Touchless washes: the risk is chemical

Nothing contacts the paint, so physical marring is off the table entirely.

The trade-off is that removing dirt without agitation requires chemistry to do all the
work — strong alkaline pre-treatments, sometimes acidic ones. Those strip wax and sealant
considerably faster than a pH-neutral hand wash does.

So you're not choosing between risk and no risk. You're choosing **which** risk: scratched
clear coat, or protection that needs reapplying sooner.

## Which to pick, and when

**Winter, salted roads:** touchless, ideally with an underbody rinse. The priority is
getting salt off frequently, and the underbody spray is the part that addresses actual
corrosion — covered in [underbody rinsing and rust](/blog/underbody-rinsing-and-rust/).

**Ceramic-coated car:** touchless. A coating handles aggressive detergent far better than
wax, and its main vulnerability is exactly the marring a brush wash causes.

**Dark paint you care about:** neither, if you can avoid it. Black shows brush marring more
than any other colour — [how to wash a black car](/blog/how-to-wash-a-black-car/) covers
why that's a visibility problem rather than a durability one.

**Summer, time available:** hand wash. Nothing automatic matches it.

## Touchless doesn't fully clean

Worth being honest about the limitation. Without agitation, touchless struggles with
bonded road film and the grimy band along the lower panels.

It removes loose dirt and salt well — which is the job that matters in winter. But a car
washed only touchless for months accumulates a film that eventually needs contact washing
to remove, so it works best as a winter routine punctuated by proper washes, not as a
permanent replacement.

## Avoid the self-service brush

The spinning brush at a coin-operated bay is usually worse than an automatic tunnel,
because it sits in a bucket or on the floor between uses and collects grit from everyone
who used it before you.

Use the pressure lance and your own mitt. Leave the supplied brush where it is.

## If you do use a brush wash

Rinse the car yourself first if there's a pre-rinse bay available — less grit going into
the brushes means less being dragged across your paint. And accept that on dark paint the
result will show, which is the trade you knowingly made for the time saved.
