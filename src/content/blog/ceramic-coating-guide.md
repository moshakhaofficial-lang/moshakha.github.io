Ceramic coatings are simultaneously the most oversold product in detailing and a
genuinely good one. Both things are true, and the gap between them is where people end up
disappointed.

## What it actually does

A ceramic coating forms a thin, hard, chemically bonded layer over your clear coat. In
practice:

- **Contamination bonds less readily.** Brake dust, tree sap and road film sit on top
  rather than keying into the paint.
- **Washing gets easier and faster.** Dirt releases with less contact, which — indirectly
  but genuinely — means fewer opportunities to add swirl marks.
- **Water sheets off** rather than sitting in beads, so fewer water spots.
- **Chemical resistance improves.** Bird droppings and hard water are less likely to etch
  through to the clear coat if you don't get to them immediately.
- **Gloss increases**, modestly.

Those are real benefits, and for someone who washes their car regularly they add up.

## What it does not do

**It does not prevent scratches.** This is the claim that sells coatings and it is
misleading. The layer is measured in microns — a fraction of the thickness of your clear
coat. It resists the very lightest marring and does nothing against:

- Swirl marks from washing with a sponge or one bucket
- Stone chips
- Key scratches or trolley damage
- Automatic car wash brushes

If you coat a car and then run it through a brush wash, it will swirl. The coating
changes how dirt behaves, not how hard your paint is.

**It does not make washing optional.** A coated car still needs washing — it just gets
easier.

**It does not last a decade.** Consumer coatings realistically give one to three years.
The larger figures on the box assume perfect preparation and maintenance.

## Preparation is most of the job

A coating **locks in whatever is underneath it** for its entire life. Apply it over
swirled, etched paint and you've sealed in those defects for two years.

Proper preparation is:

1. **Thorough wash** and dry
2. **Chemical decontamination** — iron fallout remover for bonded metallic particles
3. **Mechanical decontamination** — clay bar or mitt for anything still embedded
4. **Machine polishing** to correct defects — see
   [swirl mark removal](/how-to/how-to-avoid-swirl-marks)
5. **Panel wipe** with an IPA or dedicated prep solution to strip polishing oils

That last step gets skipped constantly and matters enormously. Polishing oils fill
defects temporarily and prevent the coating bonding to bare clear coat. Skip it and the
coating fails early in patches.

## Levelling: where DIY coatings go wrong

Application is straightforward — a few drops on an applicator, worked in a cross-hatch
pattern over a small section.

**Levelling is the part that goes wrong.** After application, the coating flashes and you
wipe off the excess before it cures. Timing this is the whole skill:

- **Too early** and you pull the coating off the panel.
- **Too late** and the residue cures into visible high spots — streaky, rainbow-ish
  patches that can only be removed by machine polishing.

Flash time varies with temperature, humidity and product. Work in **small sections**, in
shade, on cool paint, and watch the panel rather than the clock. Inspect at an angle
under good light before moving on, because a high spot is far easier to fix in the first
few minutes than after it cures.

## Realistic expectations

A ceramic coating is a **maintenance-reduction product**, not armour. If you enjoy
looking after your car and want washing to be faster and results to last longer, it's
worth the effort.

If you're hoping it means you can stop being careful, it doesn't — and a car that gets
[washed properly](/how-to/two-bucket-car-wash-method) without a coating will stay in
better condition than a coated car that goes through brush washes.
