Most pressure washers ship with a handful of colour-coded tips, and the instruction sheet
says roughly nothing about which ones suit a car. So people guess, and the guesses go from
harmless to paint-stripping.

The key idea is simple: **the nozzle doesn't change how much water comes out. It changes
how concentrated that force is.** The narrower the spray angle, the harder it hits a small
area.

## The usual colour code

Brands vary slightly, but the convention is widely followed:

| Tip | Angle | Use on a car |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 0° | **Never.** A pinpoint jet. |
| Yellow | 15° | Not on paint. Too aggressive. |
| Green | 25° | Rinsing, at a safe distance. |
| White | 40° | The everyday paint rinse. |
| Black | 65° | Soap application. |

**Always check your own machine's markings**, because not every manufacturer follows the
convention and some include only a few tips.

## Why the narrow tips are a problem

A tight jet doesn't just clean dirt, it can do three things you don't want:

- **Damage rubber and trim**, including seals and window gaskets.
- **Lift paint** at existing stone chips and edges, because the water gets underneath.
- **Drive water past seals**, into places like headlamp housings and door cards.

None of that cleans better, either. On a car, **wider is almost always the right answer**.

## A sensible setup

- **Soap application:** the black tip, or a [foam cannon](/how-to/foam-cannon-guide/) if
  your machine supports one. [Snow foam and shampoo](/blog/snow-foam-vs-car-shampoo/) are
  different jobs, and pre-foam is a rinse-first step rather than a replacement for washing.
- **Rinsing paint:** the 40-degree tip, held a foot or so back, sweeping rather than
  parking on one spot.
- **Wheel arches and mud:** the 25-degree tip is reasonable here, since it's not paint.
