Iron fallout remover is the most theatrical product in car care — spray it on a wheel and
it bleeds purple down the spokes. It looks like a gimmick. It isn't.

## Where embedded iron comes from

Brake dust doesn't just land on your wheels and paint. It lands **hot**, and hot metal
particles embed into whatever they hit rather than resting on top of it. Rail dust — from
industrial areas and from cars transported by train — does the same thing.

Once embedded, those particles oxidize. Rust expands slightly as it forms, which locks
the particle deeper into the clear coat. At that point no amount of washing removes it,
because it isn't sitting on the surface any more.

That's what causes the rough, gritty feel on paint that looks clean, and the orange
speckling that shows up on light-colored cars.

## The purple is the reaction

The active ingredient is a chelating agent that bonds to iron and forms a strongly
colored compound in the process. **The color appears because iron is present** — it's a
genuine indicator, not a dye added for the show.

Practically, that makes it a diagnostic. A panel that bleeds heavily had a lot of embedded
iron. A panel that stays clear didn't, and doesn't need the treatment.

## The step people skip

**Rinse before it dries.** Once the dissolved iron dries on the surface, it simply
redeposits — you've done the chemistry and then undone it.

That means working panel by panel out of direct sun, and rinsing thoroughly rather than
letting the product sit until it looks like it's finished. Longer dwell time doesn't make
it work better; it just increases the risk of it drying.

## Iron remover and clay do different jobs

Clay shears off contamination sitting proud of the surface, mechanically. Iron remover
dissolves iron that has embedded and corroded into the clear coat, chemically.

Doing the chemical decontamination **first** means the clay has far less material to
extract — which directly reduces the marring risk, since clay marring is caused by
dragging extracted grit. The lubrication rules for that step are in
[how to use a clay bar](/how-to/how-to-use-a-clay-bar/).

## Twice a year is plenty

This is a decontamination step, not a wash product. Most cars need it once or twice
annually; wheels may justify more, since they accumulate brake dust faster than anything
else on the vehicle.

If you're planning to coat your wheels, this step is mandatory beforehand — a coating
seals in whatever is underneath, including iron that's still actively corroding. That
argument is in
[ceramic coating wheels](/blog/do-ceramic-coatings-work-on-wheels/).

## And yes, the smell is normal

The sulfurous odor is a byproduct of the thiol chemistry that makes the iron bonding work.
Ventilate, avoid closed garages, and it clears once you've rinsed.
