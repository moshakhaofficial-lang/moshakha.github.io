Black cars have a reputation for being fragile. They aren't. They're honest.

## The paint isn't softer — the contrast is higher

Paint hardness varies by manufacturer and formulation, not by colour. There are hard black
paints and soft white ones.

What changes is **visibility**. A scratch works by scattering light. Against a dark
background that scattered light reads as a bright line. Against a light or metallic
background — where the flake is already scattering light in all directions — the same
scratch has almost nothing to contrast against.

The silver car next to yours has the same marks. You just can't see them.

## Which is why sunlight is the test

Swirl marks only appear under a **point light source** — direct sun, or a single overhead
lamp in a car park.

Diffuse light from an overcast sky illuminates them from every direction at once, so there
is no contrast and they vanish entirely. That's why a car looks flawless in the driveway
on a grey morning and dreadful at midday.

If you want to know what your technique is actually doing, inspect in direct sun. It's
uncomfortable and it's the only honest feedback available.

## The frequency trap

Black shows dust and water spots within a day, so the instinct is to wash more often.

More washing means more contact. More contact with mediocre technique means more marring —
so the car that gets washed twice as often ends up looking worse, and the owner concludes
black paint is delicate.

**Rinse more. Contact-wash no more often than you would on any colour.** That's the
resolution, and it's the same logic as pollen season, covered in
[pollen season car care](/blog/pollen-season-car-care/).

## The mitt matters more here

Everything in [how to avoid swirl marks](/how-to/how-to-avoid-swirl-marks/) applies to
every car. On black it applies visibly.

A mitt with deep, lofted pile holds grit away from the paint. A matted or dropped one
doesn't, and on black you'll see the result the first sunny day afterwards.

## The single highest-value change

**Use a drying aid.**

Drying is the final contact step, performed on a clean panel with no lubrication left,
and it's where black paint most often picks up the fine marks people notice later.

Spraying a lubricant onto the wet panel before the towel touches it turns a dragging towel
into a gliding one. It costs seconds per panel — the full mechanism is in
[drying aid explained](/blog/drying-aid-explained/).

## Correction works, and isn't free

Swirls are shallow scratches in clear coat. Polishing levels the surrounding clear coat and
removes them, and on black the improvement is dramatic because the contrast was so stark.

The catch is that polishing removes clear coat, which is finite. It's an occasional
correction, not a maintenance routine — which is the argument for getting the washing
right in the first place.
