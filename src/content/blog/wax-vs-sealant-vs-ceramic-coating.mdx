All three of these make water bead and paint look glossy, which is why they get compared
on the wrong axis. What actually separates them is durability and how much work has to
happen before they go on.

## The three, briefly

**Carnauba wax** — natural, warm-looking, easiest to apply and remove. Durability measured
in weeks. Degrades gracefully; you just apply more.

**Synthetic sealant** — polymer-based, slightly cooler and sharper-looking, still simple
to apply. Durability measured in months. The pragmatic middle for most cars.

**Ceramic coating** — a semi-permanent layer that chemically bonds to the clear coat.
Durability measured in years, with genuinely better chemical and heat resistance. And a
completely different preparation requirement.

## Prep is the real difference

This is the part that matters and the part product pages skip.

Wax and sealant sit **on** the paint. If the paint underneath has swirls or light etching,
they're still there — but so is your ability to change your mind in a few weeks.

A coating **bonds to** the paint and locks in whatever is underneath for its whole life. A
coating applied over swirled paint preserves those swirls, beautifully, for two years.

That's why coating jobs are labour-intensive: the decontamination and paint correction are
most of the work, and the coating itself is the last hour. The full expectation-setting is
in [the ceramic coating guide](/blog/ceramic-coating-guide/).

## What to choose, honestly

**Wax** if you enjoy doing it. There's a real aesthetic argument for the warmth of
carnauba on dark paint, and the ritual is the point for a lot of people.

**Sealant** if you want the effort-to-result ratio to make sense. Months of protection, an
afternoon of work, no correction required, and an imperfect base isn't a lasting
commitment.

**Coating** if you're willing to do — or pay for — the correction first, and you want the
washing to get genuinely easier for years.

There's also a fourth option nobody frames as one: a **wash-and-wax shampoo**, which adds
a small amount of protection during a wash you were doing anyway. It's not a substitute
for a proper application, but as a maintenance top-up between them it's the least effort
of anything here — which is why it's the sensible choice mid-winter, covered in
[protecting car paint in winter](/how-to/winter-car-paint-protection/).

## Don't wax over a coating

It won't damage anything, but it masks the coating's own water behaviour. When the wax
degrades, people read the change as the coating failing.

If you want to top up a coated car, use a coating-specific maintenance spray rather than a
traditional wax.

## Where the return actually is

Worth saying plainly: on a daily-driven car, **what you wash with and how often matters
more than which of these three is on the paint.**

A coated car washed with dish soap and a gritty sponge will look worse than a waxed car
washed properly. The protection layer is the last variable, not the first — the first is
in [best car wash soap](/guides/best-car-wash-soap/).
