Headlight restoration is the most satisfying car care job to do and the most disappointing
one to have done, because the result usually doesn't last.

## What's actually gone wrong

Modern headlights are **polycarbonate**, not glass. Polycarbonate yellows under UV, so the
factory applies a hard UV-resistant clear coat over it.

That coating is what fails first. Years of sun, road grit, bug impacts and washing break it
down, and once it's compromised the polycarbonate underneath oxidises quickly.

So the haze is two things: a failed coating, and oxidised plastic beneath it. That
distinction is the whole article.

## Why polishing alone backfires

Sanding and polishing removes the oxidation — and it removes whatever remained of the
factory UV coating with it.

What you're left with is **bare polycarbonate with nothing protecting it at all.** It
looks perfect, because you've just polished it. Then it yellows faster than it ever did
originally, because the original had a coating and this doesn't.

That's the pattern people report: excellent for a few months, then deteriorating on an
accelerating curve, worse within a year than before they started.

## The step that decides it

**Apply a new UV clear coat after the correction.**

That's it. The correction restores clarity; the coating is what keeps it. A restoration
with re-coating lasts years. One without lasts months and then accelerates.

Cheap kits that contain only sandpaper and polish are selling you the half of the job that
feels productive. The protective step is the one that matters, and it's the one most often
missing from the box.

## Doing it properly, in short

Mask the surrounding paint generously — sanding slurry on paint is its own problem. Wet
sand through progressively finer grits, polish to clarity, then wipe down with isopropyl
alcohol so the coating bonds to clean plastic rather than polish residue.

Then apply the UV coating in thin, even passes and leave it to cure undisturbed.

## Toothpaste, briefly

It's mildly abrasive, so it removes a little surface oxidation and produces a visible
improvement for a few weeks.

It doesn't touch yellowing deeper in the plastic and it provides no protection at all.
It's a demonstration of the principle, not a repair.

## When to replace instead

Surface work can't fix problems inside the unit. Replace rather than restore if:

- The lens is **crazed** with fine cracks throughout rather than merely hazed
- **Moisture** is getting inside the housing
- The **reflector coating** behind the lens has gone patchy or dull

Check for those before spending an afternoon on the outside of a headlight that needs
replacing anyway.
