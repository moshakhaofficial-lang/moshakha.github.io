Nearly everyone has done it: a generous glug of soap "to be safe", a bucket that foams like
a bath, and a car that looks fine until it dries.

The honest answer to the headline question is boring: **follow the label.** The useful
part is why.

## Concentration varies enormously

Car wash soaps aren't standardised. One brand can be formulated for a small measure per
bucket while another expects several times as much. A rule like "a capful per bucket" is
right for one and badly wrong for the next.

So the dilution printed on the bottle is the only reliable number. It's what the
surfactant balance and lubrication were designed around.

## What too much soap does

- **It's harder to rinse.** Leftover suds dry into a haze, especially on glass and dark
  paint.
- **It can degrade protection.** Strong washes strip wax and sealant faster, which shortens
  how long your protection lasts.
- **It doesn't add slickness.** Lubrication plateaus. You're paying for bubbles, not
  protection.

## What too little does

A weak mix is the bigger risk to paint. **The soap's job is to lubricate the dirt so the
mitt glides over it rather than dragging it.** Thin out that layer and grit rubs across
the clear coat. This is the same failure that causes
[swirl marks](/how-to/how-to-avoid-swirl-marks/).

## How to mix it properly

1. **Fill the bucket first, then add soap.** Adding soap to an empty bucket under a running
   hose over-foams and makes measuring hard.
2. **Measure.** A marked cap, a measuring cup, or a small mark on the bucket wall.
3. **Agitate with the water jet or by hand** until it's evenly mixed.
4. **Keep a second bucket for rinsing the mitt**, as in the
   [two-bucket method](/how-to/two-bucket-car-wash-method/).
