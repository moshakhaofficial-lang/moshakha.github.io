Chamois was the default car-drying tool for decades before microfiber largely replaced
it. The reason isn't that chamois stopped working — it's that microfiber fails more
gently when something goes wrong.

## What chamois actually does well

A quality chamois — natural or synthetic — holds a genuinely large volume of water and
wipes it away in fewer passes than most towels. Wrung out and reused, it can dry a full
car with less total wiping than an equivalent microfiber towel. On pure water-holding
capacity, it's a legitimately strong tool.

## Where it loses: the failure mode

The problem isn't absorbency — it's what happens when a chamois picks up a piece of
grit mid-wipe. A chamois is a smooth, flat sheet. Grit trapped between that sheet and the
paint gets dragged across the surface under real pressure, with nowhere for it to go.
That's a scratch risk on every pass, and it's largely invisible until you inspect the
paint closely afterward.

**Microfiber's raised pile changes this.** The loops and fibers lift grit slightly away
from direct contact with the paint as the towel moves across it, which meaningfully
reduces — though doesn't eliminate — the same risk. It's not that microfiber never traps
grit; it's that when it does, the geometry is more forgiving.

For someone drying a car in a driveway, not stopping to inspect the towel between every
pass, that forgiveness is worth more in practice than chamois's marginal absorbency
advantage.

## Where chamois still makes sense

On a large, flat, lower-risk surface — a garage floor, a boat hull, a window — chamois's
raw water-holding capacity is a genuine advantage with less of the grit-trapping downside
that matters specifically on car paint. The concern that pushed microfiber ahead is
specific to paint safety, not a universal verdict against chamois as a material.

## Natural vs synthetic chamois

Natural (leather) chamois is softer and more absorbent but needs care — it can crack if
left to dry out stiff, and needs conditioning to stay usable long-term. Synthetic
chamois is more durable and low-maintenance but generally holds somewhat less water per
pass. Either way, the grit risk described above comes from the smooth, sheet-like design
rather than the specific material.

## The practical answer

For drying a car's paint, microfiber's safety margin is why it's the default recommendation
in [best car drying towels](/guides/best-car-drying-towels/). If you already own a good
chamois, there's no harm using it for bulk water removal on large flat panels before
finishing with microfiber — just don't let it be the last thing that touches contoured
or detailed areas.
